Tissue reconstruction

When a structure seems to continue from one tissue section to the next, how can we tell whether it really connects?

Overview Volume explorer Orthogonal slices Connection evidence Plane-stack comparison Discussion

The question

Following a structure through the cut

A thin section cuts across a vessel and leaves a profile on the slide. One slice later, a similar profile appears nearby. Are these two views of the same vessel?

To follow a structure through depth, we align successive images so their landmarks line up, then outline the regions of interest. Stacking those outlines gives us a surface to inspect. A fold, a missing section, or a small shift in a boundary can change which regions appear to touch.

This atlas pairs each reconstruction with its source images. Begin with a synthetic branch whose shape is known, then try a measured specimen to see which connections survive a check against an independent tracing.

Imagine placing a fingertip over the second slide in the illustration. The openings above and below it give you a place to look. They leave several possibilities for the hidden section. The channel might bend, divide, or slip out of the field altogether. Lifting your finger gives you a way to check. In a real series with a missing cut, that particular check is gone.

The figures below let you move between those two kinds of view. In three dimensions you can follow a candidate path over many sections. On a single slide you can check exactly what was visible at that spot. It helps to start with a shape built expressly for that exercise.

What makes a branch?

The first specimen is a duct made for this question. Its designers gave it one root and two tips, then cut the shape into 32 simulated sections. The reconstruction had to recover the fork from the images. Having the answer in advance lets us ask exactly where it succeeds, and how little of the picture has to change before it fails.

Back at the cut surface

A section through the two arms can leave a pair of separate outlines. Taken alone, that image gives no way to tell whether the arms once shared a stem. Reading the stack in three directions

The axial image follows the plane of the section. Keep your eye on one profile as you move the section slider by a single step. In the branching phantom there is a level where one region gives way to two. You can pass through it in either direction and return to exactly the same coordinates.

Turn the stack on its side and the same spot takes on another meaning. Each row in a cut through depth comes from a different level. A profile that shifts sideways from slide to slide leaves a sloping track here. A break in that track might be a genuine interruption, or the structure might simply have curved out of the plane you chose.

The two views below take that side cut in perpendicular directions. Move one crosshair and watch the other image change. A feature that vanishes in one orientation can persist just a few rows away.

Where two sections meet

Consider just one step along the reconstructed path. On the earlier section a region occupies a patch of pixels. On the later section another region covers some of the same positions after alignment. Where their voxels share a face across the two levels, the program records a contact. That small, local observation is the building block for a much longer claim about continuity.

Look first at the images on either side of the selected interval. Is the opening in a similar position? Does the surrounding tissue give you another landmark? In a region with several small openings, a slight change in alignment can make a different neighbor seem like the best continuation.

    The synthetic series assigns four micrometres between sections. That spacing gives the depth axis a defined scale. The next example asks what we can still learn when a dataset preserves the sequence of the slides but leaves their spacing uncertain.

    Following the margin of a pancreas section

    These 36 sections come from Bijoyeta Roy’s 10× pancreas series. Start at the edge where the pink tissue meets the pale glass. A notch or a narrow projection can be easier to recognize in the next image than a patch of densely stained cells. It also gives us a particular place to check the alignment.

    Find your bearings. This middle section anchors the alignment. Follow the outer tissue margin until you can pick out the pale connective tissue along it.

    Find the tongue. The crosshair moves to the tip of a pale tongue of connective tissue on section 16. Its position was marked on the original slide and mapped into this aligned image.

    Follow the landmark. Section 17 shows the marked position of that same tip on the next slide. After rigid alignment, the two positions lay 10.9 preview pixels apart. Local refinement reduced that separation to 5.2 pixels. These landmarks were available when the method was chosen.

    There is a different difficulty at the far end of the series. On sections 35 and 36, a marked junction between a septum and a band remained 23.3 pixels apart after local refinement. A section can appear well aligned across much of its area while one recognizable junction still lands in different places.

    Rotate the stack below to inspect its edge. The file numbers establish the sequence, but the source gives neither a cut-to-cut interval nor a record of missing tissue. The displayed separation lets you see the order of the images. To convert the height of this stack into a tissue measurement, we would also need the distance between the original cuts.

    Discussion

    How far does the evidence reach?

    A reconstruction makes a prediction about the tissue between sections. These two cases show what happens when that prediction meets an independent outline.

    A vessel that could be followed

    In the H01 cortical capillary, the reconstructed main channel matched all 31 successive links in the reference tracing at the study’s primary overlap threshold. The same reconstruction also produced 21 extra links relative to that reference. Recovering the vessel’s course was easier than assigning every small fragment to it.

    Inspect the capillaryRead the validation report

    Those two counts describe different experiences of the same volume. A reader who starts inside the main opening can follow it through this sequence. A reader who starts on an extra fragment may follow a connection the reference does not support. Asking only whether the main channel stays intact would miss that second reader’s experience.

    What happened in the gap?

    In a separate experiment the team could answer a question that real slides often leave open. They built synthetic tissue in which one lumen left the image and a different one entered near the same position. Then they withheld seven sections. The two ends on either side of the gap made a convincing pair.

    Even with the sections correctly aligned, interpolation joined the unrelated lumens in all six synthetic blocks. The error occupied little of the total tissue volume. Yet the reconstructed path would let you travel between openings that belonged to different channels.

    A contour that missed its target

    In the prostate series, the experiment withheld pathologists’ tumor outlines at two levels and estimated them from the flanking outlines. The estimates missed nearly all of the annotated tumor at both levels. A smooth surface across a gap can conceal a large error.

    Inspect the withheld contoursRead the experiment

    Look at the lower row first. On level E the pathologist marked a large region in the lower half of the section. The interpolated estimate sits elsewhere. On the grid used to score the result, the two shared zero pixels. At level C they shared ten out of 1,875 annotated pixels. Copying the nearest measured outline also gave poor agreement.

    Several explanations remain possible in this specimen. A tumor focus can change between sampled levels. The slides can also shift or differ in orientation as they are stitched and aligned. The overlap score alone cannot distinguish those explanations. Landmarks inside the prostate would give a future experiment a separate check on alignment.

    The open question

    These cases test individual reconstructions. Whether seeing the stack improves a reader’s judgment on routine stained slides remains an open research question.

    A reader study could give two people the same series and ask whether two profiles belong to one branching structure. Both could inspect every original section. One would also have the linked volume. Across multiple readers and cases, the viewing conditions would switch, and an independent assessment would supply the reference for scoring.

    One answer worth recording is “I can follow it this far.” A reader might locate the last section on which a boundary is clear and the first on which two profiles are visible again. That brackets the uncertainty. It also gives the next person something concrete to inspect when they return to the slides.
